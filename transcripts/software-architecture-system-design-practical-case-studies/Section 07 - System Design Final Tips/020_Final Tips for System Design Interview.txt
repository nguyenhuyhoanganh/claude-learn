# Final Tips for System Design Interview

Now that we covered a wide range of system design problems, you have all the knowledge and the experience to tackle similar or completely new problems on your own. So in this lecture I want to discuss the specifics of system design interviews. If you took this course to prepare for an upcoming interview or if you think you may consider a job switch sometime in the future, you'll find this lecture very helpful. We'll first talk about the system design interview format and how to apply everything we learned in this course to these type of interviews. Then I'm going to give you five important tips on how to maximize your chance of success and avoid some common pitfalls. And finally, we'll talk about time management to ensure you never find yourself in a situation where you run out of time. So first of all, let's talk about the typical format of a system design interview. Just like in our design problems, an interviewer would usually give you a general requirement, like building a messaging service, a social media platform or something like that. And it is your job to fish out all the details from the interviewer and understand exactly what their requirement means. The purpose of this type of interview is to simulate a real situation at work where you get a high level task potentially from a non-technical business executive product manager or even client, and you as a software architect need to communicate with them to understand exactly what they need, translate it into technical language, and then design a system that a group of engineers can implement. So in this simulation, the interviewer is that person, and that is why they will most likely give you the problem in a vague and non-specific way. Besides the quality of your design, the interviewer will assess your communication skills, your ability to handle ambiguity and the large scope of the problem. And most importantly, think on the right level of abstraction. So everything that we've done in this course 100% applies to a system design interview. The only difference is the time limit, which is usually around 45 minutes. So now that we got a general idea of what to expect in a system design interview, I want to give you a few helpful tips for success. The first tip is not to panic. If you're given a problem you don't understand or haven't seen before, remember that at the beginning of the course we mentioned that memorization and copy pasting don't work in system design. So ambiguity, especially at the first interview stage, is normal and expected and the interviewer actually wants to see how you approach a problem you've never seen before as opposed to how well you memorized a solution to a popular problem. Also, remember that you have a process to handle this ambiguity, which we've already applied many times during the course. So for you this should feel like another day in the office. This leads me to the second tip, which is to communicate effectively with your interviewer to clarify the requirements and narrow the scope of the problem. If you completely don't understand the problem, ask the interviewer for an example of how a system like this can be used in the real world or for a similar service that you have used before. But even if you think you understand the problem, you still need to go through the first two stages of the design because what you think the interviewer means and what the interviewer actually has in mind may be completely different. In fact, properly clarifying the requirements can save you a lot of time and is also a big part of what you're being assessed on. Your interviewer wants to know that given a real problem, you will spend the necessary time and effort to understand the problem and not jump to an incorrect solution, costing the company a lot of money. During the clarification process, you should ask your interviewer questions, suggest potential requirements and clarify what is in scope and what is not in scope for. Interview due to the time limit. You'd be surprised how much of the problem the interviewer may leave out of scope. It's also important to point out that during the clarification process where you capture the non-functional requirements, you need to figure out if this is indeed a large scale design problem or not. Unfortunately, the term system design interview is somewhat overloaded, and in some companies that system may be just a single application, which means that you will be tested on object oriented programming principles and class design, and not on the design of large scale systems. If you think it is a large scale system, run some numbers like requests per day or storage to demonstrate to your interviewer that you're not just throwing a load balancer and sharding at every problem without verifying that it's actually necessary. The next step is documenting your design. In many cases, especially in big companies, the interviewer doesn't actually make the hiring decision, and at the end of the interview, they either take notes or snapshots of whatever you put on the physical or virtual whiteboard. So make sure that important things you discuss and agree on with the interviewer are well documented. This includes the systems requirements, the systems API, any calculations you make while estimating your system scale and most importantly, the architecture diagrams. This way the hiring manager or whoever makes the final decision can see your process of decision making and the final product in a very clear manner. As a side benefit documenting your requirements, decisions and diagrams helps organize your thoughts and ensures you don't lose track of your progress. The next important tip is to think out loud. Since system design doesn't have one right answer, it's super important for your interviewer to understand the logic behind your decisions. As a side benefit of thinking out loud is if you go in the wrong direction, the interviewer may stop you before you go too far off and even provide you with hints. You don't want to get too many hints or directions throughout the interview, but it's completely normal to get some because of the ambiguous nature of this type of interview. The final tip is to ensure that you clearly discuss the trade offs you're making at every step of the way, especially early in the last stage of the design, where we address the non-functional requirements and make the necessary optimizations for scale. High availability and performance. If you're choosing a specific type of database schema or data structure, make sure you explain to your interviewer why you're making that choice. If you decide to go with eventual consistency instead of strict consistency, make sure to clarify what benefits you're getting and what guarantees you're losing. Unlike in the real world, in a system design interview, it's okay if our system is not perfect. And that's because in this limited time we can never evaluate everything and take every detail into consideration. But it's important to demonstrate to the interviewer that if you were given those details or additional time to investigate, you would make the right decisions and trade offs. Now let's talk about another important topic, which is time management during an interview. There's really no hard rule on how much time you should spend on each part of the process, but I still want to provide you with the general guideline. Assuming a typical 45 minute interview this way, if you're going way off this timeline during an interview, you can quickly move on to the next step and catch up. The first two steps of the design, which are capturing the functional and non-functional requirements, should take roughly between 5 to 7 minutes with ten minutes in cases where there is high degree of ambiguity. Of course, if your interviewer emphasizes this part, this can go a little longer. But it's not usually the case Capturing the Systems API and the interactions between the user and our system should take another between 5 to 7 minutes. It's important to note that in a system design interview, it's not necessary to draw a complete sequence diagram unless you find it convenient. And that's only because in those interviews, time and whiteboard space are usually very limited. So in total, the first three steps should fit within a 15 minute time frame. The next step, which involves defining the general architecture direction of the design and drawing the architecture diagram, should take roughly 15 to 20 minutes. This leaves us about 10 to 15 minutes to make the final optimizations address. All the scalability, high availability and performance requirements and discuss particular parts of the system in greater detail. Again, this timeline is not a hard rule, but it's good to have it in the back of our minds so we don't end up in a situation where we run out of time and the interviewer is struggling to make a hiring recommendation for lack of data.
