# Software Architecture & Design of a Scalable Rideshare Service - Part 1

Welcome back to another lecture. In this section, we will design a highly scalable ridesharing service Users of the service who need to get from point A to point B can request a ride from our nearby partner drivers. Our job is to design a system to match the users with the drivers, collect the payments from the rider and pay the driver after taking our cut. In addition, we want to provide both the user and the driver with a map of their entire journey for their records. There are a lot of aspects in this design problem, so let's start with gathering the functional requirements by asking a few fundamental questions first. First, let's narrow down our scope and ask whether we need to design a system that processes credit card payments, issues, receipts, and takes care of the drivers payroll. The second question is whether we need to design a service that calculates the ETA from point A to point B given the driver's location. The next question is whether each driver can be matched only to one passenger or do we want to allow multiple people to share a ride? Then we have a very important question about calculating the riders fee and drivers compensation. Do we use time as the main metric or is it distance or some combination of the two? And finally, do we need to register users to our system before they can use it or they can use it ad hoc? So let's assume that after asking those questions, we have the following requirements. Registering a new user and logging in an existing user is part of our design. However, the drivers are registered manually after going through a background check and vehicle inspection, so that process is out of scope for our design. Each driver could be matched with multiple riders if both riders indicate that they are willing to share a ride and the driver has the appropriate capacity in their vehicle. Now the price of each ride factors in a flat base fee. The duration of the ride and the distance traveled. The exact formula is not even determined yet and is subject to change. But we do need to track the time accurately and the distance traveled to be used in that formula. Now our main job is to effectively find the nearest drivers to a user requesting a ride to minimize the wait time for the user and the distance a driver needs to travel to pick them up. Now we can use an external service to provide us with the ETA information based on real time traffic. It's also our job to collect the payment information from the users so we can build them and also collect the bank information from the drivers to pay them for their services. However, processing credit card payments and integrating with different banks is out of scope for our design, and we can call third party services through an external API. Now let's move on to capturing our non-functional requirements in terms of scale and scalability. We have the following requirements. Our service is global, so we have millions of users and hundreds of thousands of drivers operating in different countries in different cities. During peak hours such as weekends, holidays and local events, we may see demand spikes of up to ten times the average. So our system needs to be able to scale out quickly, but also scale in or scale down on days like holiday mornings where there is not much demand to save on our costs. In addition, we need to track our driver's location every 5 to 10 seconds, which is about 1.7 billion location messages per day that we need to handle from our drivers. Now let's talk about high availability. Our users rely on us for transportation, particularly in places without alternatives like public transportation. They also rely on us to provide services promptly so they don't miss their flight or are late for an appointment. Additionally, we have competitors offering similar services who would be happy to take our place. So we want to ensure we have no downtime and can gracefully handle traffic spikes or outages transparently to the users. So we will aim for availability of at least four nines, but we'll still strive to get zero downtime as much as possible. Finally, let's talk about performance. The first thing we want to take care of is the fast login of a user. Specifically, if the user accidentally mistyped their username, they should get an error message as fast as possible, ideally within less than 100 milliseconds. This way they can quickly correct their mistake, log in and book their ride sooner than later. Finally, when a user. Is logged in and wants to schedule a ride with a driver. We should be able to either match them with a driver or inform them that there isn't anyone to pick them up within five seconds at the 50th percentile and within ten seconds on busy times or crowded places. At the 99th percentile, all of those requirements will minimize the time our drivers are driving without getting paid and reduce the barriers for our users to get to wherever they want to be. Faster. And that's it. We now finished the first two very important steps in our design, which give us a clear path forward to design exactly what our customers need. So now let's move ahead and capture our system's API and sequence of events. Our system's actors are the drivers and the riders, which we'll call users. When a registered driver logs in, they get an authentication token in response from our system to their device for further communication. Then once the driver is ready to start picking up riders, they click the join button, which adds them to the pool of available drivers in that area. Then the driver's mobile app establishes a long lasting connection with our system, through which the app sends us the location of the driver every five seconds. The coordinates that indicate the driver's location are called latitude and longitude, and they come directly from the GPS of the driver's mobile device. As long as we keep getting location updates from the driver, our system keeps that driver in the pool of available drivers for that location. Now, when a new user that hasn't yet registered with our system needs a ride, their app will prompt them to choose a username and a password, as well as some details about their credit card, information, age and optionally, a profile picture. Our system stores all that information and assuming the username is not already taken and the password is strong enough, we send them a confirmation to their app. After that, the user can use those credentials to log in and get back an authentication token. At this point, the users can request a ride by sending a request containing their current location. That location is also taken from their GPS and comes in in the form of their latitude and longitude coordinates. Our system then looks for all the nearby drivers who are either free to pick up a rider or are about to finish a ride very close to the user. Then our system picks the driver that is closest to the user based on distance and traffic and matches them with the user at that point. Our driver gets a message from our system about the user's location, their name and the trip's destination. Notice that for our system to send this message to the driver's app, the connection that the driver established with our system needs to be over a bidirectional communication protocol. Now, in response to the user's request, our system sends back the driver's information, such as their vehicle color make and model license plate number and the driver's identity. While the driver is making their way to the user and keeps sending us location updates, our system keeps sending those updates to the user all the way until they meet and start their ride. This should indicate to us that the users also have to establish a bi directional communication with our system, otherwise we won't be able to send them those updates. Once the driver and the rider meet, the driver presses the start the right button on their app, which in turn sends us a request informing us that the ride has started. After the ride officially started, the driver's device keeps sending us location updates throughout the trip until they reach the destination or the driver finishes the ride manually. Once our system gets the trip completion event, it will send a confirmation to both the rider and the driver that their trip is now complete. Finally, our system will calculate asynchronously how much the user needs to pay and how much the driver will be compensated. Then it will contact the external payment system to charge the user's credit card on file. And once we get the confirmation from the credit card processing company that the user was successfully billed, will generate the map of the rider's journey and send them an email that contains that receipt and also the map of their completed trip. Similarly, our system will send a payment to the driver's bank account, and once the driver gets their payment, they will get an email with their payment details and also the map of their journey. So now, based on the sequence diagram, it's very clear how our system needs to function and more importantly, what our API looks like both for the driver app and for the riders app. So before we move on to the next step, I want to introduce another design diagram which represents all the states a driver goes through. This diagram is called a state diagram. A state diagram is very useful when we have an actor or an entity that takes different properties or is treated differently based on its current state. For example, in our case, when the driver registers to our platform, they start with the logged out state. In that state, they're not getting paid and are not eligible for rights. Once they log in and prepare to start taking rights, they transition to the available for rights state. In this state, they're also not getting paid but are available to pick up riders. Then when they get matched with a user, they are still not getting paid but are no longer eligible to be matched with another user. After they pick up the rider and start the ride, they transition to the in trip state where they are getting paid for their time but are also eligible again to be matched with users for future rides. In other words, if the driver is about to finish the trip and is very close to a user that requested a ride, we can already match that driver to this user even though they're still technically unavailable. This way we can minimize the time the driver is driving or waiting without making any money. Now, if there is no other user to match to the driver while they're still in trip, when the trip completes, the driver will automatically transition to the available for ride state where they can wait to be matched with another rider. And if they decide not to take any more rides, the driver can transition to the logged in state and later to the logged out state. As we can see, using this state diagram, we can clearly see all the state transitions for the driver and also what our system can and cannot do while the driver is in each state. And that's it. Now that we've captured all the requirements, created the sequence and state diagrams and defined our system's API, we're ready to move on to the next stage of our design. So in the next lecture we will start addressing the functional requirements with an architecture diagram that follows our Systems API.
