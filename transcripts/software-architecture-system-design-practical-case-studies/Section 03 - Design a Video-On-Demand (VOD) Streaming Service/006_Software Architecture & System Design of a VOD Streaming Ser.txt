# Software Architecture & System Design of a VOD Streaming Service - Part 1

Welcome back. In this section, we will design a highly scalable video on demand streaming platform. Our goal is to design a system that allows content creators to upload and store large video files on our system and allow users to search for and watch those videos. By the end of this section, you will know how video streaming on the internet works and you'll be comfortable designing any system that involves storage delivery or sharing of video content at scale. So as always, let's gather our system's functional requirements first. The first observation we need to make is we have two types of actors in our system content creators and users. So let's start narrowing down our scope by asking some questions about the content creators first. The first question we may ask is whether the content creators can modify their video content after they upload it to our system or not. Then we can ask what kind of information do we need to allow the content creators to provide about the video besides the video name itself? Do we need to support only video files as inputs or live streams as well? And finally, is there any particular video format that we will get from the content creators? Or it can be any format and codec? Now on the user side, let's ask the following questions. After the video has been ingested, what type of attributes can the users use to search for a video? Then we can ask, can any user watch any video or only registered or subscribed users? What kind of devices do we need to support and what other actions can the users perform besides searching and watching videos? So let's assume that after asking those questions, we have the following functional requirements. A content creator should be able to upload any type of video file, both in terms of video format and codec, but live streaming is currently out of scope. Once the video is uploaded, it can be deleted but cannot be modified. Each video should have a title, its author, its description, and an optional list of categories or tags. All those fields can be updated at any time. And finally, once the video becomes available for users to stream the content, creator should receive an email notification. On the user side, we have the following functional requirements. When we talk about a user in our system, we're talking about registered users who paid for our service. However, the payment process is outside of the scope of this design. Such users should be able to search for a video by using free text search query that considers any of the video attributes such as the video title description and so on. And finally, a user should be able to watch those videos on any device, either using a web browser or a dedicated mobile app under varying network conditions. Now, in addition, let's assume that we have the following non-functional requirements, starting from the content creators. Let's assume we're talking about a few thousand content creators that upload new content once a week at most. So the number of network requests is not very high. However, each video may be a few hours long. It may be captured at a very high resolution up to 60 frames per second and potentially may come directly from a camera, which means it's either not compressed at all or not optimally encoded for storage or streaming. So let's assume that on average each video is about 50GB. Now, in terms of fault tolerance, we do want to provide high availability so that content creators don't experience any significant downtime. However, since they're not direct consumers and they shouldn't expect immediate availability of their video to the users anyway, we can agree on an SLA of three nines on the performance side, we are going to guarantee 500 milliseconds of page load time for the content creators UI at the 99th percentile. But since we're dealing with a high variety of video sizes, some of them being very big, we will promise that the video will become available to the users in a few hours at most. Finally, in terms of data consistency, we are going to prioritize consistency over availability in the case of a network partition. This way, content creators will always see the most up to date information about their uploaded videos or none at all. Now let's move on to the non-functional requirements for the users. In this case, we're dealing with hundreds of millions of users browsing, searching and watching videos on our platform. We also have to provide high availability so our paid users get the services that they are paying for. In particular, we don't want to have a situation where our system crashes while someone is watching a video. So we will guarantee four nines of uptime externally and thrive for even higher availability internally. When it comes to performance, we need to make sure that users can get search results within 500 milliseconds at the 99th percentile. Most importantly, we need to strive towards zero buffering time when they're watching a video, regardless of the device size and network conditions. As long as their network connection is good enough to stream a video even at a very low bit rate, we should be able to deliver it to them. Finally, when it comes to data consistency, in this case we are okay with prioritizing A. Reliability over consistency for video search results. This way even if users aren't getting the most up to date video information, that's better than giving them nothing at all. Okay. Now that we captured all the requirements, let's formalize our API using a sequence diagram. In this case, we have two actors in our system, the content creators and the end users. So let's start with the content creators. After a content creator registers and logs into our system, they should be able to get a web page where they can upload their video on that page. They can also provide all the information about the video, such as its title description, its author, and an optional list of categories or tags. Our system then processes all that data and asynchronously sends an email notification to the content creator when the video becomes available for the users. Now let's proceed to the interactions between the users and our platform, since we don't need to worry about handling payments. Once the user logs into our system, they should get a user's home page where they can search for videos. Once they enter a search query, a request will go from their app or web browser to our system. In response, our system should send back a list of video titles and their ID's sorted by relevance to the search query. Of course, this is a great place to use API pagination since we don't want to send all the search results that match that query just one subset at a time. And if a user wants to get more results, they should be able to click on the appropriate button to send another request for the next batch of results. Once the user decides on the video they want to watch, they will click on the video, which should trigger a request to our system. In response, we should return either a web page with a video player to play that video in the browser or just the video URL if the client is using a dedicated app. And finally, once the user clicks the play button, a request will go to our system, which will send a continuous stream of video and audio content to the user. And that's it. That pretty much covers all our use cases. So to recap, we now have all the functional and non-functional requirements for both the content creators and the viewers, and we also have the API that we provide to both entities. Thanks to this detailed sequence diagram that we created for all our use cases. So in the next lecture we will go a lot deeper into some of the requirements we captured specifically regarding the ingestion and delivery of video to millions of users under varying network conditions and types of devices. I'll see you guys in the next lecture.
